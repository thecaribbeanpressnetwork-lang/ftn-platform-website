Where business and government meet

A Caribbean enterprise may see one payroll, one supply chain and one community. Taken together, those decisions shape where jobs grow, how money circulates and which communities can attract investment. Government sees the same economy through budgets, procurement and public services. We work at the point where those decisions affect one another.

The butterfly effect in public policy

We start from the premise that business, government and communities are connected. The butterfly effect describes how small differences at the start can lead to very different outcomes in some complex systems. In strategy, it prompts a practical question: where could one well-placed change improve the decisions that follow?

Consider a hypothetical TT$0.001 million (TT$1,000) pilot to standardise how recurring drainage complaints are logged. A council could see which sites repeatedly flood and test whether its maintenance schedule addresses them. Better records could inform procurement, budget priorities and, if the pattern holds across municipalities, national drainage policy.

We trace each step and ask what evidence would support it before recommending a wider rollout.

What this means for a long-term investment

For a project that depends on public contracts, start with three questions. Is funding approved for the full term? How long can the project carry delayed payments? What happens to the investment if refinancing costs rise? The debt figures give those questions context. The budget allocation, contract terms and cash-flow model help answer them.

Illustrative case · Retail expansion

Would the new location pay for itself?

In this hypothetical expansion, the landlord’s leasing brief, the broker’s market assessment and the company’s internal sales forecast all predict strong demand. Each relies on the same shopper survey. Before signing a lease, we’d calculate the sales needed to break even and look for independent evidence that the location could reach that volume.

See how we’d assess it

Business economics uses different models for different questions. Contribution margin shows what each sale adds toward fixed costs. Break-even estimates the sales needed to cover those costs. Discounted cash flow compares costs and returns that arrive at different times.

QBE = F ÷ (P − V)

Break-even units equal fixed costs divided by the contribution per unit. With TT$120,000 in monthly fixed costs, a TT$100 selling price and TT$60 in variable costs per sale, the location needs 3,000 sales a month to break even under those assumptions.

Our recommendation in this example would be to test local demand with a short pilot before signing the lease. The break-even threshold gives the pilot a target, and the pilot gives management evidence independent of the original survey.

Research and methods

We use applied mathematics to make assumptions explicit, compare scenarios, and see which conclusions change when the inputs change. In our exploratory research, ideas from quantum science help frame questions about observation and uncertainty. We test those ideas against evidence before drawing conclusions for a project.

Evidence-Bounded Retrodiction

EBR asks how an AI system can reconstruct possible explanations for a past event while keeping three things distinct: what was recorded at the time, what evidence shows a person could access then, and what later evidence helps explain. It preserves contradictions and leaves unresolved questions open.
