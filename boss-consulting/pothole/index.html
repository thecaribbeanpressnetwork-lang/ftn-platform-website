Where the effects can travel

Choose a branch to see what might change and what you could measure. Each is a possibility to test.

Vehicles

A wheel hits the edge → a tyre or rim is damaged → the vehicle goes in for repairs.

What to track
Punctures, bent rims, alignment, suspension and steering repairs, towing, invoices and days off the road.

Traffic

Drivers brake or wait to pass → a queue builds → journey times become less predictable.

What to track
Peak and off-peak travel times, queue length, lane changes and traffic diverted onto side streets.

Road safety

Drivers move around the hole → their paths cross those of cyclists, pedestrians or oncoming vehicles.

What to track
Observed evasive movements, near misses, falls, collisions, injuries and incident locations.

Public transport

A maxi-taxi slows or changes route → passengers wait longer or walk farther to a stop.

What to track
Run times, missed connections, trips completed, fares paid and walking distance.

Household budgets

A car needs repair → the household pays for parts and alternate transport → less money is available that week.

What to track
Repair bills, extra fares, income lost during downtime and spending deferred.

Daily life and access

A rough crossing or an unreliable ride → a journey takes too much effort → someone gives up a trip.

What to track
Missed appointments, delayed school pickups, trips abandoned and detour distance for wheelchair users or people with pushchairs.

Workers and employers

A delayed commute → a shift starts late → a colleague stays longer to cover.

What to track
Clock-in times, paid overtime, missed shifts, jobs completed and variation by route.

Schools and care

A school transport run falls behind → pupils arrive late → caregivers rearrange their morning.

What to track
Late arrivals, minutes of instruction missed, pickup times and caregiver hours.

Freight and food

A delivery van diverts → bread reaches the shop after the morning rush → some stock goes unsold.

What to track
Dispatch and arrival times, kilometres, fuel, damaged goods, spoilage and returned stock.

Shops and customers

Drivers bypass the block → fewer people stop → purchases move elsewhere or never happen.

What to track
Foot traffic, transactions by hour, stock availability, sales at nearby shops and trips made to find substitutes.

Service businesses

A technician loses time between calls → the last appointment is rescheduled.

What to track
Travel time between jobs, cancellations, repeat trips, fees and productive hours lost.

Emergency and public services

A difficult stretch slows a response vehicle or a collection truck → the next stop is reached later.

What to track
Dispatch-to-arrival intervals, route logs, missed collections and repeat service visits.

Neighbouring streets

Drivers choose a quieter street → traffic and noise shift to another community.

What to track
Before-and-after vehicle counts, speeds, noise readings, complaints and road wear on the alternate route.

Fuel and air quality

Idling and detours add engine time → fuel use can rise even for the same delivery.

What to track
Idle minutes, litres consumed and extra distance. Label emissions calculated from fuel as estimates.

Road and drainage

Water collects in the damaged area → the edge deteriorates further → the eventual repair may grow.

What to track
Dated photographs with scale, rainfall, drainage inspections, defect dimensions, repair scope and repeat failures.

Insurance and fleets

Repeated damage → more claims or spare vehicles needed → the cost of keeping a fleet available rises.

What to track
Claims, excess payments, replacement-vehicle hire, fleet availability and maintenance cost per kilometre.

Council budgets and public trust

The same defect draws repeat reports → inspection and temporary patching use staff time → residents ask when the repair will hold.

What to track
Unique defects versus repeat reports, work orders, crew hours, repair costs, response time and resident feedback.

Investment and national policy

Comparable evidence across several roads → councils can compare the wider cost of delay → ministries can reconsider how repairs are prioritised.

What to track
Comparable route studies, traffic volume, access to essential services, repair costs, budget decisions and outcomes after repairs.

Trace one outcome backwards

Morning sales fell. Was bread missing from the shelf? If so, did the van arrive late? Did it leave the bakery late, or lose time on the road? Compare till records, dispatch times and route logs before attributing the loss to the pothole.

For an economic estimate, compare similar days and an unaffected route. Count a cost once. A purchase moved to the next shop is a loss to one trader, but it may still be a sale in the same community.
